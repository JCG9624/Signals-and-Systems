Fourier view: steady-state harmonics

The PWM contains a DC component and harmonics at n fPWM. Each harmonic is multiplied by the motor's frequency response. Its voltage amplitude is 2Vdc|sin(πnD)|/(πn).

nVoltage peakCurrent peakSpeed peak

Peak (not RMS) amplitudes of individual sinusoids in the periodic steady state. These are not start-up contributions. Fourier is especially convenient here because each harmonic can be handled independently.

The mathematical connection

Electrical approximation (speed fixed)
HRL(s) = 1/(Rtot + sLtot)
fc = Rtot/(2πLtot)
Complete voltage-to-current transfer
Hi(s) = (Js+b)/[(Rtot+sLtot)(Js+b)+kekt]

Here Rtot = R + Rf and Ltot = L + Ladd. A sinusoidal voltage harmonic at frequency n fPWM produces a sinusoidal current harmonic at the same frequency, scaled and phase-shifted by Hi(j2πnfPWM). For motor speed, use Hω(s) = kt/[(Rtot+sLtot)(Js+b)+kekt].

DC versus harmonics: the opposing load torque influences the DC operating point, while fixed load torque contributes no AC harmonic. The input-to-output frequency response therefore describes deviations around the operating point.

Physical interpretation: the ideal inductor stores and returns energy (average inductor power is zero in periodic steady state). The resistances turn electrical energy into heat. A larger Ladd usually reduces ripple heating; a real inductor may add winding loss through Rf. Illustrative switching-device dissipation is added separately using an adjustable energy-per-edge assumption; actual switching loss depends on the driver, its voltage, current and transition times. Magnetic-core, brush, acoustic and other mechanical losses are not calculated. At lower frequencies, PWM harmonics may excite audible vibration; sound is not simulated.

What is the model doing?

(L + Ladd) di/dt = vPWM(t) − (R + Rf)i(t) − keω(t)
J dω/dt = kti(t) − bω(t) − TL

Start-up: the current reacts quickly to the ON/OFF transitions; accumulated torque accelerates the shaft more slowly.

Back EMF: as ω increases, keω opposes the applied armature voltage, reducing the mean current and driving the motor towards a steady speed.

Model comparison: the dashed curve uses the one-pole approximation with V̄ = DVdc. It is a good approximation to the slow speed response, not to the switching-frequency current ripple.

Frequency-domain view: for voltage-to-current and voltage-to-speed analysis, with Ltot = L + Ladd, Rtot = R + Rf and Δ(s) = (Rtot + sLtot)(Js + b) + kekt, the transfer functions are Hi(s) = (Js + b)/Δ(s) and Hω(s) = kt/Δ(s).

Drive assumption: the source is an ideal, actively driven 0/Vdc PWM terminal voltage (including a 0 V freewheel state); signed armature current is allowed. A real nonsynchronous diode/freewheel stage or current limit can change the waveform.
